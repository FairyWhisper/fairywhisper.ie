Someone noticed a thing...

November 19, 2026

Someone noticed a thing and didn't stop asking questions.

That's it. That's the history of science, of society as we know it. Archimedes didn't stop when he saw displaced water. Copernicus didn't change where we are in the universe, he noticed it and challenged accepted knowledge. Plato saw humans, human interaction, fairness and justice, and didn't stop at “this is justice because this is how we do justice.”

A flower is a flower. A flower is also the combined evolutionary strategy of a species, a mechanism for reproduction, a biological function of the plant. It's the colours of light it absorbs and those it spits back out, the chemistry of the scent it emits. A product of soil conditions, of chemistry, of physics. A flower is the culmination of perseverance, nutrition, and health of the plant, the plant is the culmination of the perseverance, nutrition, and health of the soil, and so on…

That humble little flower is the interface between the plant and its pollinators. It's a name for a little girl, or for an old lady. A gift or an apology. Would not a rose, by any other name still smell as sweet? Well, yes, it would, because the meanings we have conferred upon flowers are arbitrary human constructs that barely glance at the deep complexity and importance of the thing we have named, gifted, or put in a vase. A yellow rose may signal your apology for tardiness, but it represents so very much more than that.

In my own way, I'm discussing the nature of science, here. Science isn't, and never has been, a collection of knowledge. Science is the process by which we pursue understanding. Scientists by their very nature “notice a thing and don't stop asking questions.” The very best scientists in history have *refused* to stop asking questions.

Children are natively scientific. Real children. Ordinary children. And yet, by the time they reach adulthood, most have learned the required knowledge to get them through school and forgotten the science part (Anniston hair swish moment).

Loathe, as I am, to admit it, Dan Brown popularised the vision of DaVinci as a scientist and inventor. Leo, as with all the great renaissance artists, were observers, questioners, and testers along their paths. And I hate to break it to you, that's science.

One of the many disservices we do to ourselves, as individuals and en masse, is to “other” science, to insist that science exists only in a dearth of art. Science is the art of intrigue. A disciplined approach, yes, but as are schools of art, design, and thought. While I compile the handbook, the code club, my own university work, I have been repeatedly hit with this generalised idea that science and art live two separate lives, while repeatedly seeing proof of this lie. I find myself battling with the expectation I've put on myself to learn knowledge, rather than seek understanding and foster further questioning.

There is beauty beyond the visible. Yes, there is beauty in the understanding. Beyond the surface, below it. But don't forget there is beauty in the questions too.

Granted, I'm not claiming Archimedes streaking down the village shouting about puddles was a pretty sight to see …
